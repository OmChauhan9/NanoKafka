Each `Partition.append` writes the record and calls `FileChannel.force(true)` on the log, then opens the index, writes 16 bytes and forces it. An append costs 4,125 us at 100 B and 4,709 us at 1 KB. The same two forces with no Partition code, the force pair row, cost 3,872 us, which is 96% of an append measured in the same repetition (range 89% to 102%).

A lone force costs 2,571 us, so two lone forces would be 127% of an append. The pair is cheaper than two lone forces (3,872 us against 5,142 us): the second, 16-byte force right after the first costs less than a lone one.

Bytes do not set the pace. At 1 KB the log takes 213 KB/s, far below any local disk's sequential bandwidth, and the 1 KB rate is 88% of the 100 B rate. The appending thread kept 0.12 cores busy, so it spends most of its time blocked in the kernel. More concurrent forces raise the device's total rate only 1.35x at 4 files and 2.85x at 16.

Limiter: fsync latency. The log and index forces alone account for 96% of an append's time.

The 2-segment partition is built with `Partition.append`. The 11- and 20-segment partitions are written straight to disk in the same format, because building them by append would take minutes of fsyncs, and then opened with `new Partition`. The setup row proves the direct writer produces the same bytes as `append`, rollover included.

Across 2, 11 and 20 segments and the first, middle and last segment, median p50 latency spans 23.1 to 23.3 us (a ratio of 1.01). The widest run-to-run range of any one cell is 0.92 us.

Verdict on the README's "index-based O(1) message lookups": flat within noise on this range. Read latency does not grow with the offset's position or with segment count from 2 to 20.

Scope of that verdict. From the code, each read binary-searches the segment list, which is O(log S), then calls `segments.indexOf`, which is O(S), then seeks straight to the index entry, which is O(1) within a segment. At 20 segments those list walks cost nanoseconds, so this run cannot separate O(1) from O(log S) or O(S) in the segment count. What it shows is that a lookup never scans log bytes before the target offset. Behavior at thousands of segments is inconclusive.

Limiter: opening files. Every read opens and closes the index file and then the log file. One isolated open, size, read and close costs 10.8 us, so two of them account for 94% of the 23.2 us p50 read in the 20-segment partition's last segment.

Inconclusive: cold reads. Every file here was just written, so reads hit the OS page cache. Dropping the cache needs root, so disk-bound read latency was not measured.

| fetch one 100 B message by offset | p50 us | 118 | 110 to 162 | 0 | 1.28 | 1500/1500 returned the bytes produced at that offset |
| fetch one 100 B message by offset | p99 us | 139 | 131 to 267 | 0 | 1.28 | same calls as the p50 row |

Leaders are broker 1 for `lat-rf1` and broker 1 for `lat-rf2`. The client opens a new TCP connection for every request (`Protocol.call`). A metadata request does no disk work, so its 122 us p50 is the cost of one round trip on a fresh connection.

Produce at replication factor 1 is 6,048 us at p50. An in-process append measured in the same repetitions is 5,863 us, so the append is 97% of the produce latency and the round trip floor adds 2% more. Limiter: the leader's two fsyncs, as in scenario a.

Produce at replication factor 2 is 10,730 us at p50, 1.77x the factor 1 latency. From the code, `replicateToFollowers` submits the copy to an executor and the leader acks before the follower answers, so the ack path has no extra network round trip, and an acked message may not be on the follower yet when the client sees the ack. Both brokers share one disk, and scenario a shows concurrent forces add little device throughput, so the follower's two fsyncs likely delay the leader's. Two appends' worth of forces is 11,726 us, which matches 109% of the factor 2 p50. Limiter: fsyncs from both replicas queued on one device (inferred from that arithmetic, not profiled).

Fetch is 118 us at p50. The in-process read from scenario b is 23.2 us and a metadata round trip is 122 us, together 122% of the fetch. Limiter: the request round trip on a fresh connection, not the disk; the read itself comes from the page cache.

Scenario wall time 27.3 s. Warnings or errors logged by NanoKafka during it: 0.

Each message is 100 B at replication factor 1, and each rep sends 240 messages. Thread i sends to partition i mod P with its own client. Every client and both brokers share one JVM and one disk. Topic `conc-p4` placed partition 0 on broker 1, partition 1 on broker 2, partition 2 on broker 1, partition 3 on broker 2.

`Partition.append` holds the partition's write lock across both fsyncs, so at most min(threads, partitions) appends run at once. The ceiling below is the interleaved force pair rate at that many threads, since each append does one log force and one index force. With one client thread the partition sits idle during every request's round trip. With more threads another request is already waiting on the lock.

| Variant | Measured msgs/s | Concurrent appends | fsync ceiling msgs/s | Share of ceiling |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 thread, 1 partition | 155 | 1 | 232 | 67% |
| 4 threads, 1 partition | 174 | 1 | 232 | 75% |
| 16 threads, 1 partition | 184 | 1 | 232 | 79% |
| 4 threads, 4 partitions | 232 | 4 | 253 | 92% |
| 16 threads, 4 partitions | 253 | 4 | 253 | 100% |

Limiter: inconclusive for the variants below 70% of their fsync ceiling (lowest 67%). Something besides fsync, such as connection setup per request or lock handoff, costs the rest, and this run does not separate them.

The load generator was not the limit. The whole JVM, clients and brokers together, kept at most 0.34 of 11 cores busy.

Every request opens a new TCP connection and the client closes it, leaving a TIME_WAIT socket. This machine's ephemeral port range and TIME_WAIT length allow about 546 new connections/s sustained. The fastest variant used 46% of that. Here the disk saturates first. On a disk with faster fsync, sustained produce above that rate would fail with port exhaustion.

Scenario wall time 48.3 s. Warnings or errors logged by NanoKafka during it: 0.
